# Company and brand history profile

## Audience and promise

English-speaking general viewers interested in how familiar companies were built. Assume curiosity rather than business training. Explain terms such as royalties, licensing, network effects, and vertical integration through an example before using them repeatedly.

The reference niche is the public **Built On You** channel, `https://www.youtube.com/@BuiltonYou`. Its public description identifies chronological histories of major businesses, founder origins, controversies, and the people behind them. Inspect its current uploads before claiming particular titles, durations, performance, or visual conventions. This profile is an original editorial interpretation of that niche; it does not imply affiliation or access to channel analytics.

## Story choices

Choose a recognizable company and a mechanism with a visible explanation: franchising, flatpack logistics, component manufacturing, licensing, subscriptions, distribution, or a developer ecosystem. A topic is stronger when the viewer can see a decision cause a later success or problem.

Useful structure, adapted to the story:

1. A concrete contradiction or consequential moment that establishes the central question.
2. Origins, including the founders and the conditions around them.
3. The first workable product and customer need.
4. A diagram of how the business actually scales or earns money.
5. Expansion, competition, and a strategic fork.
6. A documented failure, limitation, or controversy, with attribution and proportion.
7. The response and the legacy at a dated endpoint.

Do not force every story into the same seven chapter lengths. A full script needs complete spoken prose, a payoff, and transitions, not chapter summaries. For roughly 20 minutes, start near 2,500–2,900 spoken words at 130–150 words per minute and recalculate after the actual read. Shorter documentaries are valid; label them honestly.

## Packaging

Titles can identify either the historical scope or the central mechanism. Avoid copying a reference channel's exact title. Do not promise the “entire” history when the episode deliberately selects a narrower question. Do not include a duration that the actual script cannot support.

Descriptions should state the subject, time period, central question, and useful search terms naturally. Avoid unsupported superlatives and claims of secret knowledge. Explain controversies without treating allegations as verdicts. Add source links and media credits in the eventual upload description.

## Research discipline

- Record the precise claim, historical period, source URL, supporting section, and source type. Company archives establish what a company reports; independent primary records can test it.
- Keep legal entities separate: Samsung Group is not Samsung Electronics; the McDonald brothers' restaurant predates Kroc's company; a brand owner and its bottlers have different economics.
- Separate revenue, profit, market capitalization, total system sales, and transaction volume. Show the year and units on every financial visual.
- Address commonly repeated myths when relevant: IKEA did not invent flatpack furniture; Netflix's origins should not be reduced to an unsupported late-fee anecdote; company founders and later executives are not interchangeable.
- Prefer a dated historical endpoint to unsourced “today” figures. Recheck current ownership, leadership, counts, litigation, and prices immediately before production.
- Discuss labor, consumers, creators, suppliers, and communities when the mechanism materially affects them. Avoid a hero-only story or a preset moral judgment.

## Visual language

Use original SVG scenes, object close-ups, clearly dated timelines, maps, and mechanism diagrams. A modern documentary look is the default recommendation, not a claim about how the reference channel is made. The inherited dusk-lit sets are optional illustration assets, not a requirement that every boardroom be dark. Use enough on-screen dates and labels to orient the viewer. Make charts readable at phone size and leave time to read them.
